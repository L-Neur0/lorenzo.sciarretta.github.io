Neural network optimization / Weight-space learning

Can AI help us train
better neural networks?

From a new dataset to useful model weights.
Generate a starting model. Then fine-tune.

Imagine building a visual-inspection model for a new product. You have labeled images and a CNN architecture; you need weights that recognize defects. Today, you might adapt a pretrained checkpoint or train with SGD or Adam. The ambition: use AI to generate useful starting weights for your dataset, then fine-tune them—so each new task can build on what other networks have already learned.

Current research explores two routes into weight space. SANE (2024) learns representations of trained networks and decodes sampled embeddings into new weights. Weight-Space Programming (2026) explores general-purpose LLMs reading, editing, and writing network weights through reasoning. Both investigate how AI can help create or improve the network itself, alongside conventional training.

Our contribution: a step toward scalable model generation. SANE-MoE adds expert specialization to the SANE autoencoder. In our mixed-zoo experiments, it improves weight reconstruction and the accuracy of generated small CNNs. Learning from broader model collections is the motivation; combining this backbone with dataset conditioning and scaling to larger networks are future directions.

The idea in one picture

Trained models become the training data.

A model zoo is a collection of trained checkpoints. SANE learns to encode and reconstruct their weights, processing windows of weight tokens instead of a whole network at once.

Where the optimizer fits. Training SANE itself still uses optimization. Once trained, it can generate starting weights for a compatible network; SGD or Adam can then adapt those weights to your data. Sampling, batch-normalization calibration, and candidate evaluation are part of the practical pipeline.

Our contribution / SANE-MoE

Diverse networks. Specialized experts.

Weights from different layers and datasets need not follow the same patterns. We replace feed-forward sublayers in selected SANE Transformer blocks with a router and multiple expert networks. The router can use token content, position, and layer information to choose which experts process each weight token.

We study top-k, position-aware, and hierarchical routing. The aim: retain useful structure when the model zoo contains networks trained on different datasets.

What we measured

Better generated checkpoints in a mixed zoo.

Trained on a mixture of small CNN zoos from CIFAR-10 and STL-10, every evaluated MoE routing variant improves mean generated-model accuracy over dense SANE.

What this demonstrates—and what comes next

Our experiments support better reconstruction and generation in this small-CNN, mixed-zoo setting. They do not establish a universal pretrained initializer, generation for arbitrary architectures, or a smooth latent space organized by function. Testing larger networks, broader model zoos, and end-to-end fine-tuning efficiency remains future work.

Conclusion / Toward scalable weight-space learning

Better weight representations.
Better generated networks.

Our method improves the SANE backbone: expert routing yields more faithful weight reconstruction and higher generated-model accuracy across both datasets in the mixed-zoo evaluation. This is a step toward learning from broader collections of neural networks and generating useful model weights at larger scale.

Future direction · conceptual pipeline

Dataset + compatible architecture → dataset-aligned weight space → generated NN weights → task-specific fine-tuning

WeightCLIP (2026) supplies a complementary idea: align dataset information with weight embeddings. Combining dataset conditioning with an expert-based backbone is a research direction, rather than a result established in our report. Scaling to larger architectures and measuring the complete training benefit are the next tests.
